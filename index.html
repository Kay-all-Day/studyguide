Risk Assessment Methodologies

QUALITATIVE · QUANTITATIVE · STRIDE · DREAD

▾

Core Definition

  • Risk = Threat × Vulnerability × Impact — the fundamental risk equation
  • Threat: A potential event that can cause harm to assets
  • Vulnerability: A weakness that can be exploited by a threat
  • Impact: Potential damage or loss if a threat exploits a vulnerability
  • Likelihood: Probability that a threat will exploit a vulnerability

Quantitative Risk Assessment — Key Formulas

Core Calculations
SLE = Asset Value × Exposure Factor (EF)
ALE = SLE × ARO
ALE (after control) = SLE × new ARO
Annual Savings = ALE (before) − ALE (after)
Net Benefit = Annual Savings − Cost of Control
→ Implement if Net Benefit > 0
SLE

Single Loss Expectancy — monetary loss from ONE occurrence of a risk event.

ARO

Annual Rate of Occurrence — how often an event occurs per year (e.g. 0.25 = once every 4 years).

ALE

Annualized Loss Expectancy — expected annual financial loss from a risk. Used to justify security investments.

Exposure Factor

Percentage of asset value lost in a single incident (0–1). EF of 0.20 = 20% of asset value destroyed.

💡 Worked Example from Lecture: SLE = $120,000, ARO = 0.25 → ALE = $30,000. After control (ARO → 0.05): ALE = $6,000. Saving = $24,000. Control costs $15,000. Net benefit = $9,000 → Implement the control.

Qualitative vs Quantitative

AspectQualitativeQuantitative
BasisSubjective expert judgementNumerical data, financial metrics
OutputHigh / Medium / Low ratingsALE, SLE, ARO values
SpeedFastSlow (requires data)
Best forSmall orgs, initial evaluationLarge orgs, financial justification
ToolsRisk matrix, brainstormingHistorical data, calculations

Threat Models

S — SpoofingT — TamperingR — Repudiation I — Info DisclosureD — Denial of ServiceE — Elevation of Privilege

STRIDE (Microsoft) — DREAD: Damage, Reproducibility, Exploitability, Affected Users, Discoverability

Risk Mitigation Strategies

  • Avoidance: Eliminate the risk by removing the vulnerability entirely
  • Transfer: Shift risk to a third party (insurance, outsourcing)
  • Mitigation: Reduce risk via security controls (most common)
  • Acceptance: Acknowledge and accept if mitigation cost > potential loss

Cryptography

SYMMETRIC · PUBLIC-KEY · RSA · EL GAMAL · ABE

▾

Core Terminology

Plaintext

Original readable message (m)

Ciphertext

Encrypted, unreadable output (c)

Cipher

Algorithm transforming plaintext → ciphertext

Key

Secret info used in cipher, known only to authorised parties

Cryptography

Study of encryption principles and methods

Cryptanalysis

Breaking ciphertext without knowing the key

Symmetric (Private-Key) Cryptography

  • Same key used for both encryption and decryption
  • DES: Data Encryption Standard — published by NIST in 1977, now obsolete
  • AES: Advanced Encryption Standard — published by NIST in 2001, current standard
  • Kerckhoffs's Principle: Algorithm is public; only the key is secret
  • Shift Cipher: cᵢ = [mᵢ + k mod 26] — only 26 keys, trivially broken by brute force
  • Key Distribution Problem: Parties must share key via a secure channel before use
  • Key Management Problem: N users need O(N²) keys — not scalable

RSA — Key Generation Process

RSA Step-by-Step
1. Choose large primes p and q
2. Compute n = p × q (public modulus)
3. Compute φ(n) = (p-1)(q-1) (Euler's totient)
4. Choose e: 1 < e < φ(n), gcd(e, φ(n)) = 1
5. Find d: e × d ≡ 1 (mod φ(n)) ← extended Euclidean
Public key: (e, n) | Private key: (d, n)
Encrypt: C = Mᵉ mod n
Decrypt: M = Cᵈ mod n
  • Security based on Integer Factorization Problem (IFP) — hard to factor large n
  • Minimum 2048-bit keys today; 3072+ bits recommended post-2030
  • Timing attack: measure decryption time to infer key bits → use constant-time + blinding
  • Textbook RSA is deterministic → use OAEP padding (PKCS#1 v2) in practice
  • Vulnerable to Shor's quantum algorithm → driving post-quantum cryptography research

El Gamal Encryption

El Gamal Setup & Encryption
Setup: prime p, generator g, private key x
Public key: y = gˣ mod p
Encrypt M with random k:
c₁ = gᵏ mod p
c₂ = M × yᵏ mod p
Ciphertext: (c₁, c₂)
Decrypt: s = c₁ˣ mod p, M = c₂ × s⁻¹ mod p
  • Security based on Discrete Logarithm Problem (DLP)
  • Probabilistic: same plaintext encrypts to different ciphertexts (random k) → semantically secure
  • Ciphertext is twice the size of plaintext (a pair)
  • Never reuse k in signatures — exposes private key x

Public-Key vs Symmetric — Key Comparison

SymmetricRSA / El Gamal
Keys1 shared secret keyPublic/private key pair
Key sharingRequires secure channelPublic key freely distributed
Scale (N users)O(N²) keysN key pairs only
SpeedFast (AES ~1000× faster)Slow
Use caseBulk data encryptionKey exchange, signatures
Hard problem–IFP (RSA) / DLP (El Gamal)

Advanced PKC — ABE

  • Attribute-Based Encryption (ABE): encrypt to users matching attributes — one-to-many
  • KP-ABE: ciphertext has attribute set; key has tree-policy; decrypt if attributes satisfy policy
  • Introduced by Sahai & Waters, CCS 2005
  • Applications: cloud storage, IoT, eHealth records, encrypted access control

Business Continuity & Disaster Recovery

BCP · DRP · BIA · MTD · RTO

▾

Key Definitions

  • BCP: Business Continuity Planning — maintaining operations during emergencies with reduced capability
  • DRP: Disaster Recovery Planning — restoring operations AFTER continuity is broken
  • BIA: Business Impact Assessment — identifies critical resources, threats, and financial impact
  • MTD: Maximum Tolerable Downtime — longest acceptable outage for a function
  • RTO: Recovery Time Objective — target time to restore; must be < MTD
  • ARO: Also used in BIA likelihood assessment, expressed as annualised rate

BCP Four-Step Process

Step 1

Project Scope & Planning — business analysis, team formation, resource assessment, legal review

Step 2

Business Impact Assessment — identify priorities, risks, likelihood, and impact (quantitative + qualitative)

Step 3

Continuity Planning — strategy development, provisions, processes, plan documentation

Step 4

Approval & Implementation — senior management sign-off, deployment, training, education

🏆 Top Priority: People safety ALWAYS comes first. IT recovery and infrastructure restoration come after ensuring personnel are out of harm's way.

BCP Team Composition

  • IT and Security departments (not exclusively)
  • Legal counsel — ensures regulatory compliance
  • Operations departments — real-world process insight
  • Senior management — authority, resources, endorsement

Laws vs Ethics

  • Laws: carry authority of governing body; enforceable; drawn from ethics
  • Ethics: define socially acceptable behaviour; no legal authority
  • Cultural mores: fixed moral attitudes of a particular group — basis of ethics

  • Due diligence: ongoing, valid effort to protect others

Policy made readily available to all employees

2. Review

Distributed in intelligible form (multilingual, accessible)

3. Comprehension

Employee demonstrated understanding (quizzes, assessments)

4. Compliance

Employee agreed to comply (logon banner, signed document)

5. Uniform Enforcement

Applied consistently regardless of employee status

Web Security

XSS · SQL INJECTION · PHISHING · DRIVE-BY · MITM

▾

Browser Attack Types

  • Man-in-the-Browser: Trojan infects browser as an add-on/helper object; intercepts all browser data (SilentBanker 2008)
  • Keystroke Logger: Records all keystrokes; can be hardware (USB dongle) or software malware
  • Page-in-the-Middle: Redirects user to attacker-controlled page
  • Program Download Substitution: Replaces legitimate downloads with malware
  • Drive-By Download: Malware silently downloaded without user interaction; exploits browser/plugin vulnerabilities

Injection Attacks

XSS — Stored

Malicious script permanently stored on server (e.g. forum post); executes for every visitor

XSS — Reflected

Script embedded in URL; executes when victim clicks link (common in phishing)

XSS — DOM-Based

Script injected into the DOM and executed client-side by JavaScript

SQL Injection

Injects SQL into application-database exchange; e.g. OR '1'='1' bypasses authentication

Dot-Dot-Slash

Directory traversal using ../ to access unauthorised files on server

SSI Injection

Server-Side Include directives injected via text input to execute server commands

Phishing Taxonomy

TypeDescription
Mass PhishingLarge-volume attack targeting as many people as possible
Spear PhishingTargeted, uses personal info from social media; 84% of orgs penetrated (2015)
WhalingSpear phishing targeting senior executives (CFO, CEO, SVP)

Countermeasures

  • Sanitise and validate ALL user input; trust nothing, check everything
  • Content Security Policy (CSP) to restrict script execution sources
  • DMARC + SPF + DKIM to prevent email spoofing
  • Keep browsers, OS, and plugins fully updated
  • STOP → THINK → CONNECT before clicking any link

Q1

RSA Key Generation

FOUNDATIONAL · 8–10 MARKS

▾
A financial institution wishes to implement RSA encryption. Given the primes p = 11 and q = 13:

(a) Compute the public modulus n and Euler's totient function φ(n).
(b) Choosing e = 7, verify this is a valid public exponent and compute the private exponent d using the Extended Euclidean Algorithm.
(c) State the complete public key and private key.
(d) Why must p and q be kept secret once n is published?
💡
Hint: Valid e requires gcd(e, φ(n)) = 1. For d, solve e×d ≡ 1 (mod φ(n)) by back-substituting the Extended Euclidean Algorithm. Verify by checking e×d mod φ(n) = 1.
Model Solution
(a) Computing n and φ(n):
    n = p × q = 11 × 13 = 143
    φ(n) = (p-1)(q-1) = 10 × 12 = 120

(b) Verifying e = 7 and finding d:
    gcd(7, 120): 120 = 17×7 + 1 → gcd = 1 ✓  (valid exponent)
    
    Extended Euclidean to find d such that 7d ≡ 1 (mod 120):
    120 = 17 × 7 + 1
    1   = 120 - 17 × 7
    
    Therefore d ≡ -17 ≡ 120 - 17 = 103 (mod 120)
    
    Verification: 7 × 103 = 721 = 6 × 120 + 1 ✓

(c) Public key:  (e, n) = (7, 143)
    Private key: (d, n) = (103, 143)

(d) Why p and q must stay secret:
    If an attacker knew p and q, they could recompute φ(n) = (p-1)(q-1)
    and then recover the private exponent d = e⁻¹ mod φ(n), completely
    breaking the encryption. The security of RSA depends entirely on
    the difficulty of factoring n = p×q back into p and q. Knowing
    either prime collapses this hard problem to a trivial computation.
Q2

RSA Encryption & Decryption

APPLIED · 10 MARKS

▾
Using the RSA keys from Q1 (public key e=7, n=143; private key d=103, n=143):

(a) Encrypt the message M = 5 to produce ciphertext C.
(b) Decrypt C to verify you recover M = 5. Show all modular arithmetic using repeated squaring.
(c) Encrypt M = 5 again without any padding scheme. What does this reveal about textbook RSA, and what standard padding scheme is used in practice?
💡
Hint: C = Mᵉ mod n. Use repeated squaring: express the exponent in binary (7 = 111₂, 103 = 1100111₂) and square-and-multiply. For (c), think about what happens when you encrypt the same plaintext twice.
Model Solution
(a) Encryption: C = M^e mod n = 5^7 mod 143
    Using repeated squaring (7 = 4 + 2 + 1):
    5^1 mod 143 = 5
    5^2 mod 143 = 25
    5^4 mod 143 = 625 mod 143 = 625 - 4×143 = 53
    5^7 = 5^4 × 5^2 × 5^1 = 53 × 25 × 5 mod 143
         = 1325 × 5 mod 143
         53 × 25 = 1325 mod 143 = 1325 - 9×143 = 38
         38 × 5  = 190 mod 143 = 47

    Ciphertext: C = 47

(b) Decryption: M = C^d mod n = 47^103 mod 143
    103 in binary = 64 + 32 + 4 + 2 + 1
    47^1   mod 143 = 47
    47^2   mod 143 = 2209 mod 143 = 64
    47^4   mod 143 = 64² mod 143 = 4096 mod 143 = 92
    47^8   mod 143 = 92² mod 143 = 8464 mod 143 = 27
    47^16  mod 143 = 27² mod 143 = 729 mod 143 = 14
    47^32  mod 143 = 14² mod 143 = 196 mod 143 = 53
    47^64  mod 143 = 53² mod 143 = 2809 mod 143 = 92
    
    47^103 = 47^64 × 47^32 × 47^4 × 47^2 × 47^1
           = 92 × 53 × 92 × 64 × 47 mod 143
           
    Step-by-step:
    92 × 53  = 4876 mod 143 = 4876 - 34×143 = 4876 - 4862 = 14
    14 × 92  = 1288 mod 143 = 1288 - 9×143  = 1288 - 1287 = 1
    1  × 64  = 64
    64 × 47  = 3008 mod 143 = 3008 - 21×143 = 3008 - 3003 = 5

    Decrypted: M = 5 ✓

(c) Determinism problem:
    Encrypting M=5 again produces C=47 — exactly the same ciphertext.
    This reveals textbook RSA is DETERMINISTIC: identical plaintexts
    always produce identical ciphertexts, leaking whether two messages
    are the same. This fails semantic security (IND-CPA).
    
    Practice standard: OAEP (Optimal Asymmetric Encryption Padding)
    — standardised in PKCS#1 v2.x. OAEP adds a random seed before
    encryption, making each encryption unique even for identical
    plaintexts, and prevents chosen-ciphertext attacks.
Q3

RSA Security Analysis

ANALYTICAL · 12 MARKS

▾
Critically analyse the security of RSA encryption:

(a) What computational hard problem underpins RSA's security? Why is it considered hard?
(b) What minimum key length is recommended for RSA today and why has it increased over time?
(c) Describe the timing attack on RSA and explain two mitigations.
(d) Describe the threat of quantum computing to RSA and name one post-quantum alternative scheme.
💡
Hint: Think about the GNFS algorithm for factoring, Shor's algorithm for quantum, and constant-time / blinding techniques for timing. Key lengths increase because computing power grows (Moore's Law) and factoring algorithms improve.
Model Solution
(a) Hard Problem — Integer Factorisation Problem (IFP):
    Given a large semiprime n = p×q, find p and q.
    No polynomial-time classical algorithm is known. The best 
    classical algorithm (General Number Field Sieve, GNFS) runs in 
    sub-exponential time — practical for small n but infeasible for 
    2048+ bit numbers. The asymmetry makes multiplication fast but 
    inversion (factoring) computationally intractable.

(b) Key Length Recommendations:
    Minimum TODAY: 2048 bits.
    Recommended post-2030: 3072–4096 bits.
    
    Why lengths increase:
    — Computing power grows (Moore's Law); larger keys needed to 
      maintain equivalent security margin.
    — Factoring algorithms improve (GNFS, SNFS); each advancement 
      reduces effective security of existing key sizes.
    — 512-bit RSA: broken in 1999. 1024-bit: breakable with nation-state 
      resources. 2048-bit: secure against classical computers today.

(c) Timing Attack (Kocher, 1996):
    Mechanism: RSA decryption time varies based on private key bit 
    values. An attacker measures hundreds of decryption times 
    statistically to infer bits of the private key d.
    
    Mitigation 1 — Constant-time implementation:
    Ensure all decryption operations take identical time regardless of 
    key bits (eliminate all data-dependent branches and memory accesses).
    
    Mitigation 2 — RSA Blinding:
    Before decryption, multiply ciphertext by r^e mod n (random r).
    Decrypt (r^e × C)^d = r × M mod n, then divide by r to recover M.
    The random r prevents timing information from correlating with d.

(d) Quantum Threat — Shor's Algorithm:
    Peter Shor (1994) showed a quantum computer can factor n = p×q in 
    polynomial time O((log n)³), completely breaking RSA.
    A sufficiently large quantum computer (thousands of logical qubits) 
    would render all current RSA-encrypted communications vulnerable.
    
    Post-Quantum Alternative — CRYSTALS-Kyber (NIST selected 2022):
    Based on the hardness of the Module Learning With Errors (MLWE) 
    problem — believed resistant to both classical and quantum attacks.
    Other options: CRYSTALS-Dilithium (signatures), FALCON, SPHINCS+.
Q4

El Gamal Encryption

APPLIED · 10–12 MARKS

▾
The El Gamal cryptosystem uses prime p = 23, generator g = 5, and private key x = 6.

(a) Compute the public key y.
(b) Encrypt message M = 10 using random ephemeral key k = 3. Show ciphertext (c₁, c₂).
(c) Decrypt (c₁, c₂) using the private key to verify recovery of M.
(d) How does El Gamal's probabilistic nature differ from textbook RSA, and what security advantage does this provide?
💡
Hint: y = g^x mod p. Encrypt: c₁ = g^k mod p, c₂ = M×y^k mod p. Decrypt: s = c₁^x mod p, M = c₂×s⁻¹ mod p. For modular inverse, use Fermat's little theorem: s⁻¹ = s^(p-2) mod p.
Model Solution
(a) Public Key y = g^x mod p:
    y = 5^6 mod 23
    5^1 = 5
    5^2 = 25 mod 23 = 2
    5^3 = 5 × 2  = 10
    5^6 = 10^2   = 100 mod 23 = 100 - 4×23 = 8
    
    Public key: y = 8
    Full public key: (p=23, g=5, y=8)

(b) Encryption of M=10 with k=3:
    c₁ = g^k mod p = 5^3 mod 23 = 125 mod 23 = 125 - 5×23 = 10
    
    Shared secret: s = y^k mod p = 8^3 mod 23
    8^1 = 8, 8^2 = 64 mod 23 = 18, 8^3 = 18×8 = 144 mod 23 = 6
    
    c₂ = M × s mod p = 10 × 6 mod 23 = 60 mod 23 = 60 - 2×23 = 14
    
    Ciphertext: (c₁, c₂) = (10, 14)

(c) Decryption using private key x=6:
    s = c₁^x mod p = 10^6 mod 23
    10^2 = 100 mod 23 = 8
    10^3 = 8 × 10 = 80 mod 23 = 11
    10^6 = 11^2 = 121 mod 23 = 121 - 5×23 = 6
    
    Find s⁻¹ mod 23:
    By Fermat's little theorem: s⁻¹ = s^(p-2) mod p = 6^21 mod 23
    (Shortcut: test 6×4 = 24 ≡ 1 mod 23 → s⁻¹ = 4)
    
    M = c₂ × s⁻¹ mod p = 14 × 4 mod 23 = 56 mod 23 = 56 - 2×23 = 10 ✓
    
    Recovered: M = 10 ✓

(d) Probabilistic vs Deterministic:
    RSA (textbook): Deterministic — same plaintext M always produces 
    same ciphertext C = M^e mod n. This violates semantic security; 
    an attacker can test if two ciphertexts encrypt the same message.
    
    El Gamal: Probabilistic — random ephemeral key k means M=10 
    encrypted with k=3 gives (10,14) but with k=7 gives a completely 
    different pair. An attacker cannot distinguish encryptions of the 
    same message.
    
    Security advantage: El Gamal achieves IND-CPA (semantic security 
    under chosen plaintext attack) in the standard model, assuming the 
    Decisional Diffie-Hellman (DDH) problem is hard. Textbook RSA 
    requires padding (OAEP) to reach the same security level.
Q5

Digital Signatures & Non-Repudiation

ANALYTICAL & APPLIED · 12 MARKS

▾
Using the El Gamal parameters from Q4 (p=23, g=5, x=6, y=8):

(a) Explain conceptually how El Gamal digital signatures work, including the roles of the signing key and verification key.
(b) How does the key usage in digital signatures differ from El Gamal encryption? Explain why this reversal achieves non-repudiation.
(c) A developer reuses the random k=3 for two different message signatures. Explain in detail why this is catastrophic and how an attacker could exploit it to recover the private key x.
(d) Compare El Gamal signatures with RSA signatures in terms of the underlying hard problem and signature size.
💡
Hint: For (c), if you have two signatures (r, s₁) and (r, s₂) on messages h₁ and h₂ with the same k, then s₁-s₂ = (h₁-h₂)/k mod (p-1). Since r is the same, k is exposed, and then x follows from s×k = h - x×r.
Model Solution
(a) El Gamal Signature Scheme:
    Signing (using private key x):
    1. Compute hash h = H(message)  [e.g. SHA-256]
    2. Choose random k: gcd(k, p-1) = 1
    3. r = g^k mod p
    4. s = (h - x×r) × k⁻¹ mod (p-1)
    5. Signature = (r, s)
    
    Verification (using public key y):
    Check: g^h ≡ y^r × r^s (mod p)
    
    Rationale: Only someone knowing x can produce a valid s for a 
    given r and h, because s requires knowledge of x and k. The 
    verification equation is designed so only valid (r,s) pairs satisfy it.

(b) Key Role Reversal and Non-Repudiation:
    
    ENCRYPTION: Public key → encrypt | Private key → decrypt
    Alice encrypts to Bob using Bob's PUBLIC key; only Bob can decrypt.
    Purpose: confidentiality (only recipient reads).
    
    SIGNATURE:  Private key → sign   | Public key → verify
    Alice signs with her own PRIVATE key; anyone verifies with her PUBLIC key.
    Purpose: authenticity + non-repudiation.
    
    Non-repudiation: Since only Alice possesses her private key x, only 
    she could have produced a signature that verifies under her public 
    key y. She cannot later deny signing the message — the signature is 
    cryptographically bound to her identity. Courts can accept this as 
    evidence of intent.

(c) Catastrophic k-Reuse Attack:
    
    Suppose Alice signs two messages M₁ and M₂ with the SAME k=3:
    — Both signatures have the SAME r = g^k mod p (since r depends only on k)
    — s₁ = (h₁ - x×r) × k⁻¹ mod (p-1)
    — s₂ = (h₂ - x×r) × k⁻¹ mod (p-1)
    
    Attacker observes (r, s₁) and (r, s₂) and knows h₁, h₂:
    
    s₁ - s₂ = (h₁ - h₂) × k⁻¹ mod (p-1)
    
    → k = (h₁ - h₂) × (s₁ - s₂)⁻¹ mod (p-1)
    
    Once k is recovered, from s₁ = (h₁ - x×r) × k⁻¹:
    → x = (h₁ - s₁×k) × r⁻¹ mod (p-1)
    
    The ENTIRE private key x is exposed. Real-world example: Sony's PS3 
    used a fixed k for firmware signing — hackers recovered the private 
    key and were able to sign arbitrary code, enabling piracy.
    
    Mitigation: Use a cryptographically secure random k for EVERY 
    signature, or use RFC 6979 deterministic k-generation.

(d) El Gamal vs RSA Signatures:
    
    Hard Problem:
    — RSA signatures: Integer Factorisation Problem (IFP) — hard to factor n
    — El Gamal signatures: Discrete Logarithm Problem (DLP) — hard to find x given g^x mod p
    
    Signature Size:
    — RSA: signature is one value, same size as n (e.g. 256 bytes for 2048-bit key)
    — El Gamal: signature is a PAIR (r, s), each ~same size as p — roughly 2× RSA
    
    Determinism:
    — RSA signatures (with PSS padding): deterministic or randomised
    — El Gamal: inherently randomised (requires fresh k each time)
    
    In practice, ECDSA (Elliptic Curve DSA — an El Gamal variant) gives 
    smaller signatures than RSA at equivalent security, making it preferred 
    for constrained environments (mobile, IoT, TLS certificates).

(a) Asymmetric (Public-Key) Cryptography

Definition:
Asymmetric cryptography uses TWO mathematically related keys — a PUBLIC
key and a PRIVATE key — generated as a matched pair. Data encrypted with
the public key can ONLY be decrypted with the corresponding private key,
and vice versa. The public key is freely shared; the private key is kept
secret by its owner.

Diagram:

  Sender (Alice)                           Receiver (Bob)
  ─────────────                            ─────────────
  Plaintext ──► [Encrypt with Bob's     ──► Ciphertext ──► [Decrypt with Bob's  ──► Plaintext
                 PUBLIC Key]                                 PRIVATE Key]

  Key Generation (Bob):
  ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  Key Pair Generator                           │
  │                                               │
  │  Public Key  ──► Shared openly with anyone   │
  │  Private Key ──► Kept secret by Bob only     │
  └──────────────────────────────────────────────┘

How it works:
1. Bob generates a public/private key pair.
2. Bob shares his PUBLIC key openly (anyone can see it).
3. Alice encrypts her message using Bob's PUBLIC key.
4. Only Bob's PRIVATE key can decrypt the ciphertext — nobody else can.

Key Properties:
• Computationally infeasible to derive the private key from the public key.
• Solves the key distribution problem of symmetric cryptography.
• Slower than symmetric encryption due to complex mathematical operations.

Two Examples of Asymmetric Encryption Algorithms:
1. RSA (Rivest–Shamir–Adleman): Security based on the difficulty of
   factoring the product of two large prime numbers. Used in TLS/SSL,
   digital signatures, and secure email.
2. El Gamal: Security based on the Discrete Logarithm Problem (DLP) in
   a finite group. Used in GPG/PGP encryption and as the basis for DSA
   (Digital Signature Algorithm).


(b.i) The CIA Triad

The CIA Triad is the foundational model of information security, comprising
three core principles that guide the protection of information assets:

┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                        CIA TRIAD                              │
│                                                               │
│   ┌──────────────┐  ┌──────────────┐  ┌──────────────────┐  │
│   │CONFIDENTIALITY│  │  INTEGRITY   │  │  AVAILABILITY    │  │
│   │               │  │              │  │                  │  │
│   │ Only authorised│  │ Data is      │  │ Systems and data │  │
│   │ parties can   │  │ accurate and │  │ are accessible   │  │
│   │ access data   │  │ unmodified   │  │ when needed      │  │
│   └──────────────┘  └──────────────┘  └──────────────────┘  │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

• CONFIDENTIALITY: Ensures that sensitive information is accessible only
  to those authorised to view it. Techniques include encryption, access
  controls, and data classification. Prevents unauthorised disclosure.

• INTEGRITY: Ensures that data and systems remain accurate, complete, and
  unaltered by unauthorised parties. Techniques include hashing, digital
  signatures, and checksums. Protects against unauthorised modification.

• AVAILABILITY: Ensures that information systems and data are operational
  and accessible to authorised users whenever required. Techniques include
  redundancy, backups, and DDoS mitigation. Prevents denial of service.


(b.ii) CIA Triad Breach Examples in a Financial Institution

• CONFIDENTIALITY Breach:
  An attacker uses SQL injection to extract the customer database, exposing
  thousands of account numbers, PINs, and personal details. Unauthorised
  parties can now access private customer financial records.

• INTEGRITY Breach:
  A malicious insider modifies transaction records in the core banking
  system to redirect funds into their own account. The financial data is
  no longer accurate or trustworthy — its integrity has been violated.

• AVAILABILITY Breach:
  A Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attack floods the bank's online
  banking portal with millions of bogus requests, crashing the servers.
  Legitimate customers cannot access their accounts or perform transfers.

(a) Symmetric (Secret-Key) Cryptography

Definition:
Symmetric cryptography uses a SINGLE shared secret key for BOTH encryption
and decryption. The same key must be securely distributed to all authorised
parties before communication can take place.

Diagram:

  Sender (Alice)                                  Receiver (Bob)
  ─────────────                                   ─────────────
  Plaintext ──► [Encrypt with       ──► Ciphertext ──► [Decrypt with       ──► Plaintext
                 Shared Secret Key]                     Shared Secret Key]

  Secure Key Exchange (must happen beforehand):
  ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │          ← ← ←  Shared Secret Key  → → →            │
  │  Alice                                       Bob     │
  │  (holds key)                           (holds key)   │
  └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

How it works:
1. Alice and Bob agree on a shared secret key (via a secure channel).
2. Alice encrypts plaintext using the shared key → produces ciphertext.
3. Bob decrypts the ciphertext using the same shared key → recovers plaintext.
4. Anyone who intercepts the ciphertext without the key cannot read it.

Key Properties:
• Much FASTER than asymmetric encryption — suitable for bulk data.
• Key distribution is a challenge: the shared key must be exchanged securely.
• Both parties must trust each other and protect the key equally.
• Does not provide non-repudiation (either party could have encrypted it).

Comparison with Asymmetric:
• Symmetric: 1 key (shared), fast, key distribution problem.
• Asymmetric: 2 keys (public + private), slow, no distribution problem.
In practice, asymmetric cryptography is used to securely exchange a
symmetric session key (hybrid encryption — e.g., TLS).

Two Examples of Symmetric Encryption Algorithms:
1. AES (Advanced Encryption Standard): Block cipher operating on 128-bit
   blocks with key sizes of 128, 192, or 256 bits. NIST-standardised in
   2001. The dominant symmetric algorithm worldwide; used in TLS, disk
   encryption (BitLocker, FileVault), and Wi-Fi (WPA2/WPA3).
2. DES (Data Encryption Standard): Block cipher with a 56-bit key,
   standardised in 1977. Now considered insecure due to its short key
   length (broken by brute-force in 22 hours in 1999). Superseded by
   3DES (Triple DES) and eventually AES.

(c.i) Policy vs Law

POLICY:
A policy is a formal document created by an organisation (e.g. a company,
government agency, or institution) that establishes rules, guidelines,
standards, and expected behaviours for individuals within that organisation.
Policies are internally created, communicated, and enforced.

Example: "All employees must use strong passwords and change them every
90 days" — this is an organisational security policy.

LAW:
A law is a rule enacted by a recognised governing body (legislature or
parliament) with jurisdiction over a geographic area or sector. Laws apply
to all persons and organisations within that jurisdiction, regardless of
their internal policies.

Example: Ghana's Data Protection Act 2012 (Act 843) legally requires
organisations to process personal data lawfully and protect it appropriately.

Key Differences:

  Dimension        Policy                    Law
  ───────────────  ────────────────────────  ─────────────────────────────
  Created by       Organisation internally   Government / legislature
  Applies to       Members of organisation   Everyone in the jurisdiction
  Enforcement      HR / management action    Courts, regulators, police
  Consequences     Disciplinary / dismissal  Fines, prosecution, imprisonment
  Binding nature   As a condition of work    Mandatory regardless of consent

In summary: a policy is voluntary in the sense that employees agree to it
as a condition of employment; a law is mandatory and cannot be opted out of.
Both can coexist — a company policy may exceed the minimum standard set
by law.


(c.ii) Liability in Information Security

Definition:
Liability is the LEGAL RESPONSIBILITY of an individual or organisation to
compensate for harm, loss, or damage caused to another party as a result
of an action, inaction, or failure to exercise reasonable care.

In the context of information security, an organisation may be held liable
when it fails to adequately protect the sensitive data entrusted to it,
resulting in harm to affected parties (customers, employees, or partners).

Types Relevant to Information Security:

1. Negligence-Based Liability:
   The organisation failed to implement a reasonable standard of care.
   Example: A bank stores customer passwords in plaintext. A breach
   exposes them. The bank is liable because any reasonably competent
   organisation would have hashed passwords.

2. Strict Liability:
   Liability applies regardless of intent or fault, often imposed by
   statute. Example: Data protection laws (e.g., GDPR, Ghana's Data
   Protection Act) impose liability for breaches of personal data even
   if the organisation acted in good faith.

3. Vicarious Liability:
   An organisation is responsible for the actions of its employees.
   Example: If an employee negligently emails confidential records to
   the wrong recipient, the organisation — not just the employee —
   may be held liable.

Practical Implications for Management:
• Implement documented security policies and enforce them.
• Conduct regular risk assessments and close identified gaps.
• Train employees — ignorance is not a legal defence.
• Maintain audit trails to demonstrate due diligence.
• Obtain cyber liability insurance as a risk transfer mechanism.
• Understand applicable legislation (Data Protection Act, Computer
  Crime Act) to ensure compliance and minimise legal exposure.
